Strategy, Product and Marketing Must Operate as One Growth System

Growth stalls not because individual functions are weak, but because there is no coherent chain of decisions connecting the customer, the market, the offer and the investment.

SDSanchita Dhara•2 min read

I have watched organizations produce excellent market research, innovative product ideas and sophisticated campaigns — and still struggle to turn them into sustained growth.

The problem is rarely the quality of any one function. It is the missing chain of decisions that connects them. My approach treats Strategy, Product and Marketing as one enterprise operating model, supported by independent research and a shared performance language.

Start with the strategic choice, not the campaign

Strategy must answer whom to prioritize, in which markets, with what differentiated value and for which economic objective. That means integrating competitive intelligence, demographics, household and lifecycle characteristics, transactional behavior, profitability and unmet needs. A segmentation is only useful when it changes decisions.

At TDECU, I built an enterprise segmentation roadmap that combined internal behavior with Claritas household intelligence and market opportunity analysis. The output was not a persona library; it was a framework for acquisition, retention, relationship growth and geographic prioritization.

Product translates insight into a value proposition

Product should decide whether the priority customer needs a new capability, a simpler process, different packaging, a better price or a better experience. That takes qualitative and quantitative research, feature prioritization, willingness-to-pay exploration, journey design and commercial trade-off analysis.

At Walmart, my research and analytics informed membership features and value propositions — foundational work for what became Walmart+ — along with omnichannel and pharmacy experiences. In financial services, the same discipline connects life-stage needs with deposit, lending and digital products. The link is never “segment A gets product B” by assumption; it is a validated hypothesis about customer value.

Marketing activates — and tests — the proposition

Marketing should not be asked merely to generate impressions. It should turn the proposition into positioning, audiences, channel choices, lifecycle communications and testable offers. The measurement system must separate acquisition volume from profitable acquisition, conversion from incrementality, and engagement from long-term relationship growth.

My work has included deposit campaign measurement, mortgage acquisition analysis, acquisition cost, attribution and marketing-mix frameworks. The purpose is not to celebrate a conversion rate, but to learn which investment changed customer behavior economically.

Build decision rights around the entire chain

I run the model on a five-stage cycle: Sense → Choose → Design → Activate → Learn.

  • Strategy is accountable for segment and market choices.
  • Product owns propositions, experience and the roadmap.
  • Marketing owns positioning, activation and channel performance.
  • Analytics and Research provide independent evidence, consistent definitions and evaluation.

Weekly reviews address activation and friction; monthly reviews connect segment and product performance; quarterly reviews revisit strategic choices and resource allocation. One enterprise scorecard traces priority segment → customer need → offer → channel → adoption → value. The exact RACI is adapted to each company.

What leaders should measure

Segment penetration and growth; proposition uptake; feature adoption and customer effort; acquisition cost by segment; conversion and incrementality; retention and relationship depth; and contribution economics. When results miss plan, this chain reveals whether the issue was the strategic choice, product fit, activation or measurement.

The executive test

Can any leader pick a growth target and trace it back to a segment hypothesis, a customer need, a product decision and an activation plan? If not, the organization may have a strategy and several capable functions — but not an integrated operating model.
